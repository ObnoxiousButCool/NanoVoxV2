## Transcript

Agent Carlos: Choice Administrators, this is Carlos.
Member: Hi Carlos. Ibrahim Nasser, CHM-4416628. My neurologist prescribed Ajovy for chronic migraine. The pharmacy says it's
rejected because I need to try something called step therapy first. But I've already tried the drugs they want me to try — years ago,
with a different insurance. They didn't work.
Agent Carlos: That's a common and fixable situation. Step therapy requires trying preferred medications first, but if you've already failed
them, that history satisfies the requirement — it just has to be documented and submitted.
Member: So I don't have to take them again?
Agent Carlos: No. Making a patient repeat a failed therapy isn't the intent. What we need is documentation of the prior trials — which
drugs, roughly when, and the outcome.
Member: I took topiramate for about eight months and propranolol for six. Topiramate gave me cognitive side effects, propranolol just
didn't reduce frequency.
Agent Carlos: That's exactly the documentation needed — two preferred agents, adequate trial duration, one discontinued for adverse
effects and one for lack of efficacy. That's a textbook step therapy exception case.
Member: But those records are with my old insurance and a doctor I no longer see.
Agent Carlos: The records don't have to come from us or from the prior insurer. Your current neurologist can document the history
based on your report and their clinical assessment. A statement in the prior auth request saying 'patient has documented trial and failure
of topiramate and propranolol' is generally sufficient.
Member: That's a relief. I was worried I'd have to track down eight-year-old pharmacy records.
Agent Carlos: Not necessary. What I'd suggest is calling your neurologist's office today and asking them to submit a prior authorization
for Ajovy with a step therapy exception request, including the two prior failures and the reason each was discontinued.
Member: How long does that take?
Agent Carlos: Step therapy exceptions are typically 3 to 5 business days. And because migraine preventives are not urgent-onset
medications, there's no bridge supply mechanism — but 3 to 5 days is manageable.
Member: Okay. Anything else that helps the case?
Agent Carlos: If your neurologist can note migraine frequency — days per month — that strengthens it. Chronic migraine is defined as
15 or more headache days monthly and that classification supports the CGRP class.
Member: I'm at about 18 days a month.
Agent Carlos: Then make sure that number is in the submission. It matters. Your reference number is ST-2026-04471 — have the office
include it.
Member: ST-2026-04471. Carlos, this was genuinely useful. I expected to be told to just take the old drugs again.
Agent Carlos: That would have been the wrong answer. Good luck, Ibrahim.
